# Reusable evidence and the user's voice

## Build once, update deliberately

Store a compact `evidence.md` beside the private tracker, outside the installed skill. Start from supplied materials and update with confirmed answers. Use short evidence IDs so role assessments and draft notes can refer back to proof without copying the whole CV. IDs belong in private notes, not recipient messages.

For each useful piece of evidence record:

| Field | What to capture |
| --- | --- |
| Source | Supplied filename/link, version or date, and page/section when available. |
| Supported claim | What the candidate actually did; retain verbs that distinguish led, contributed, supported, and observed. |
| Ownership and scope | Individual responsibility, team context, project scale, dates, and metric attribution. Keep absent details unknown. |
| Demonstrated strengths | Skills supported by this example, with interpretations labelled. |
| Relevant proof | Authorized work sample or an explanation the user can give; do not assume a confidential artifact can be shared. |
| Open questions | Contradictions, missing context, or unverified claims that affect reuse. |

Separate direct evidence, candidate-confirmed clarification, and inference. A hypothetical exercise or self-initiated sample is evidence of that exercise, not paid client work. A missing portfolio does not erase demonstrated work in the CV. Suggest a sample only when it would answer a real evidence gap.

For a role, map its important responsibilities to relevant evidence IDs, gaps, and the user's constraints. Explain why the strongest evidence transfers. Do not turn a team result into individual impact, sum overlapping dates, or treat an unverified requirement as satisfied. If a newer source conflicts, ask about the affected claim; use the uncontested evidence meanwhile. Retain prior source references and retire obsolete claims so they are not reused accidentally.

## Calibrate voice when writing starts

Reuse preferences already supplied. Otherwise offer an optional short writing sample that the user owns and is comfortable sharing, or show two brief versions of the same opening: for example direct and warm. Keep the facts and purpose the same so the user is choosing a voice. Ask what feels natural and what they would never say. Avoid a personality questionnaire.

Capture useful preferences in the private brief: formality, warmth, sentence length, degree of playfulness, disliked phrases, language, and any approved example. Do not infer personality from demographic details or reproduce distinctive wording from a stranger's messages. Store only as much sample text as is needed and authorized.

If the user asks to draft immediately or skips calibration, proceed in a plain, professional voice and invite one focused correction. Calibration is not an approval gate. Adapt to the recipient and channel while preserving the user's preferred tone; sensitive replies can require more restraint than an introduction.

When the user edits a draft, identify whether the change corrects a fact, expresses a recurring preference, or is specific to this recipient. Ask only when the distinction matters. Never silently generalize a factual or one-off edit into a permanent rule. Keep the user's current draft edits intact.
