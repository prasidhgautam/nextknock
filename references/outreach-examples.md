# Annotated outreach examples

Everything below is fictional and illustrative. Names, products, events, and experience are invented for demonstration; never use them as live research or candidate evidence. The examples explain editorial choices, not templates to fill in.

## A product detail with a credible connection

Fictional inputs: CedarLoop's release note says administrators can save onboarding checklists. Jordan Vale's CV says they documented handoff problems, maintained onboarding checklists, and contributed to a team pilot; it contains no measured business result. The intended recipient leads implementation.

Weak opening: “Congratulations on your innovative launch. I am passionate about customer success and confident I can transform your onboarding.”

The praise could fit any company. The transformation claim has no supporting evidence, and the recipient gets no useful reason to respond.

Private connection: saved checklists → implementation handoffs may need local exceptions (a hypothesis) → Jordan has documented handoff issues → ask how the team balances consistency and flexibility.

One possible message:

> Subject: A question about CedarLoop's onboarding checklists
>
> Hi Sam,
>
> The saved checklists in CedarLoop's release caught my attention: they give teams a shared starting point while leaving room for different workflows.
>
> In my support role, I documented handoff issues and maintained onboarding checklists during a team pilot. That work made me curious about where a shared process helps and where it gets in the way.
>
> How does your implementation team decide what stays standard and what a customer can change? I'm exploring implementation work and would value your perspective on that tradeoff.
>
> Jordan

Why it is stronger: the appreciation has a concrete object, the proof retains the candidate's actual ownership, and one relevant question gives the recipient something specific to answer. It does not diagnose CedarLoop's performance or imply Jordan used the product.

## Relevant product evidence when recent news is absent

Fictional inputs: HarborDesk's current help page distinguishes account-owner setup tasks from end-user tasks. A support candidate has written separate administrator and user guidance. No meaningful development in the preceding year was verified.

Weak angle: “Your recent expansion is impressive.” No expansion was established; even a real expansion might have no connection to this candidate.

Better angle: “Your setup guide separates account-owner tasks from end-user steps. I made a similar split in support documentation after collecting recurring questions. How does your team decide when an issue needs a better guide versus a product change?”

This is an opening fragment, not a complete email. It uses relevant product evidence without inventing recency. Tell the user the news search found no suitable development; keep that limitation out of the recipient's message.

## Preserve voice without changing evidence

Using the same fictional checklist observation:

- Direct: “Your saved onboarding checklists caught my attention. I worked on similar handoffs and had a question about how your team handles exceptions.”
- Warm: “I liked the thought behind your saved onboarding checklists. Having worked on similar handoffs, I'm curious how your team makes room for exceptions.”

Both have the same facts and purpose. Let the user choose or edit; avoid turning either wording into a mandatory opener.
